Making a good website template or UI kit is only half the job. The other half is getting it in front of people who are looking for one.
If you design templates, build themes, or sell Figma, Framer or Webflow products, the question quickly changes from "how do I make this?" to "where do I put it?" There are dozens of answers, and they don't all do the same thing. Some places sell your product for you. Some give you a checkout and leave the traffic to you. Some never process a sale but still send you customers.
This guide covers more than 20 platforms and compares them on audience, product type, how they make money, competition, how easy they are to be found on, and how friendly they are to beginners. Fees and rules change often, so check each platform's current terms before you commit.

The three kinds of platform

1. Places that sell your product. Buyers browse, pay and download on the platform itself. Examples: ThemeForest, Creative Market, UI8, Framer Marketplace, Webflow Marketplace. You get access to shoppers, but you share the space with thousands of competitors.
2. Places that run your checkout. Gumroad, Payhip and Lemon Squeezy give you a store and handle payment and delivery. Nobody is browsing there looking for your template, though, so you bring the traffic.
3. Places that help you get seen. Behance, Dribbble, Pinterest, LinkedIn and creator directories like ThemesMotion don't take the payment. They put your work in front of people who may then go to your product page.
Most successful creators use at least one from each group.

Part 1: Platforms that sell your products

1. Envato Market (ThemeForest)

Best for: WordPress, HTML and CMS theme developers. ThemeForest is one of the best-known places to sell website themes. Buyers go there to find a ready-made site, so you don't have to explain why templates exist. The price is competition: a generic "business theme" gets lost quickly, so a niche such as dental clinics, SaaS startups or restaurants helps. Envato charges authors a fee on each sale, so work out your take-home amount before you set a price. Beginner friendliness: moderate.

2. Creative Market

Best for: Designers who sell templates alongside other assets. Creative Market covers website templates, UI kits, fonts, graphics, mockups and more. That's handy if you make more than one kind of product, because a Figma dashboard kit, a landing page template and a font can all live in one shop. Commission varies, so read the current seller terms. Beginner friendliness: moderate to good.

3. UI8

Best for: UI/UX designers selling premium UI kits, design systems and coded templates. UI8's audience is almost entirely designers, so "Figma UI kit" needs no explaining. The quality bar is high, which is good if your work is strong. Present your files, documentation and licence clearly. Beginner friendliness: moderate.

4. Framer Marketplace

Best for: Framer creators. If you build in Framer, this is the natural first stop. Shoppers are already looking for Framer websites they can customise. Good screenshots, accurate categories and a clear description matter a lot, because the competition is growing. Check Framer's current revenue terms for creators. Beginner friendliness: good, if you already know Framer.

5. Webflow Marketplace

Best for: Webflow designers and agencies. Webflow's marketplace has an approval process for templates, and it's separate from "Made in Webflow", which is a showcase for finished sites. Pick a niche and make sure the template is properly responsive. Beginner friendliness: moderate.

6. TemplateMonster

Best for: Established template creators who want another marketplace. It's a traditional template marketplace for WordPress, WooCommerce, Elementor and HTML products. Author onboarding has changed over time, so check the current application process before planning around it. Beginner friendliness: moderate.

7. Figma Community

Best for: Getting Figma work noticed. Figma Community is where people look for UI kits, wireframes and design systems. One important detail: Figma says it is currently not approving new creators to sell paid files there. New creators can still publish free files, which is a good way to build an audience, and then send people to a paid kit sold elsewhere. Beginner friendliness: good for free files, limited for paid.

8. Etsy

Best for: Business-friendly templates (social media kits, portfolio templates, brand kits). Etsy has a huge consumer and small-business audience and supports digital downloads. It suits products for entrepreneurs more than developer-focused ones. There are listing and transaction fees, so check the current rates. Beginner friendliness: good.

9. Creative Fabrica

Best for: Designers with a broad range of downloadable assets. It's better for graphics, fonts and printable templates than for advanced website products, so think of it as a secondary channel. Read the licensing terms before uploading. Beginner friendliness: moderate to good.

Part 2: Platforms that run your own store

10. Gumroad

Best for: Independent creators with an audience. Gumroad makes it quick to put a product online. There's no monthly fee: direct sales cost 10% plus $0.50, and sales that come through its Discover marketplace cost 30%. That fixed 50 cents hurts cheaper products the most, so do the maths for your price point. Its biggest strength is speed. Its biggest weakness is that it won't find buyers for you. Beginner friendliness: high.

11. Payhip

Best for: Creators who want a simple storefront with different plans. Payhip offers a free plan with a transaction fee and paid plans with lower fees, so you can start free and upgrade when you're selling enough. It also supports bundles and pay-what-you-want pricing. Beginner friendliness: high.

12. Lemon Squeezy

Best for: Independent digital-product businesses selling internationally. It handles checkout, delivery, licences and subscriptions, and is aimed at people who want more than a basic download link. As with Gumroad and Payhip, traffic is up to you. Beginner friendliness: moderate to good.

Part 3: Directories and free-theme channels

13. WordPress.org Theme Directory

Best for: WordPress developers. This is a directory of free themes, not a shop. You can't list a $59 theme there and expect a checkout. Many developers use it as the top of a funnel: a free theme brings users, and some upgrade to a paid Pro version sold on the developer's own site. Submissions are reviewed against WordPress's guidelines. Beginner friendliness: moderate.

Part 4: Platforms that help you get seen

14. ThemesMotion

Best for: Template creators, UI designers and developers who want more places to be discovered. ThemesMotion is not a marketplace for individual template purchases. It's a discovery and promotion platform. Creators can list templates (Framer, Webflow, Figma, WordPress, React and Next.js are all on the site), and visitors who like one click through to the creator's own checkout, often on Gumroad or Lemon Squeezy. The site also has free design assets such as 3D illustrations and animated icon packs, a directory of design experts available for hire, and a showcase of well-designed websites. That mix of visitors, people looking for ideas, templates and designers to hire, is useful for creators because it gives your product another way to be found without you giving up your own store. Treat it as one door into your product, alongside Behance, Pinterest and search. You can explore it at themesmotion.com, or browse its templates and free design assets. Beginner friendliness: good.

15. Behance

Best for: Turning a template into a case study. Behance is a portfolio platform. Instead of posting "buy my kit", show the thinking: the problem, the layout, desktop and mobile previews, and a link to the product. It's free and beginner friendly, though the competition for attention is high.

16. Dribbble

Best for: Visual previews of UI work. Dribbble is great for short, eye-catching shots of interfaces. It's a marketing channel rather than a store, so use it to send people to your product page.

17. GitHub

Best for: Developers. Publish a free starter or component library with a clean README and a demo link. People who find it useful may go on to buy the premium version. It builds credibility with a technical audience, but you'll want to be comfortable with Git.

18. LinkedIn

Best for: Freelancers, agencies and B2B creators. Don't post "buy my template". Explain a design decision, share a before-and-after, or break down how you built something, and link to the product as the next step.

19. Reddit

Best for: Targeted communities such as web design, Figma, Webflow and Framer. Reddit can send very relevant traffic, but each subreddit has its own rules about self-promotion, and repeated promotion gets removed. Contribute first, promote second.

20. Pinterest

Best for: Visual products that suit entrepreneurs, bloggers and small businesses. Pins keep getting found over time, unlike a feed post that disappears in a day. Make several pins per product (desktop preview, mobile preview, feature collage) and link each to the right page.

21. Product Hunt

Best for: New launches with a story. It's good for a launch moment, not for steady template sales. Use it when you have something new to announce and are ready to answer comments.

22. Your own website

Best for: Long-term growth. Over time, a site with useful articles and product pages for specific searches ("Framer templates for startups", "Figma dashboard UI kit") becomes the most valuable asset you have, because you control the traffic, the brand and the customer list.

Which platform should you choose?

Start with what you make, not with the platform.

Figma templates and UI kits: UI8 and Creative Market for sales, plus a storefront like Gumroad or Payhip. Use Figma Community for free files to get noticed. Add Behance and a directory such as ThemesMotion.

Framer templates: Framer Marketplace first. Add Dribbble, your own site and a directory listing.

Webflow templates: Webflow Marketplace first, then your own site and portfolio channels. Approved templates can sometimes be sold elsewhere too, so read Webflow's current rules.

WordPress themes: WordPress.org for a free version, ThemeForest or TemplateMonster for paid, and GitHub if you want developer credibility.

You're a beginner: don't publish everywhere. Choose one place to sell, one place to show your work and one directory to be found in. Add more once you see what works.

You have an audience already: sell from your own store. The marketplaces become a bonus.

You have no audience: a marketplace helps you get your first buyers while you build a name through Behance, Pinterest or LinkedIn.

Your goal is search traffic and backlinks: build a small network of pages, each useful on its own: your product page, a Behance project, a LinkedIn post, a directory listing and a blog article. The aim isn't to scatter links but to put your work wherever the right people already look.

FAQ

Where is the best place to sell website templates? There isn't one. Marketplaces like ThemeForest, Creative Market and the Framer and Webflow marketplaces bring buyers. Storefronts like Gumroad and Payhip give you control. The right mix depends on your tool, your niche and whether you already have an audience.
Where can I sell Figma templates? UI8 and Creative Market are the main marketplaces, and independent storefronts work well too. Figma Community is great for free files, but Figma says it isn't currently approving new creators to sell paid files there.
Where can I sell Framer or Webflow templates? Start with each tool's own marketplace, then add your own site, a storefront and discovery channels.
Can I sell templates without a marketplace? Yes. A storefront like Gumroad, Payhip or Lemon Squeezy plus a blog, social posts, a newsletter or search traffic is enough. It's slower to start but you keep control.
Are portfolio platforms worth the effort if they don't sell anything? Yes. Behance, Dribbble, Pinterest and LinkedIn bring people who then visit your product page. Think of them as part of your marketing, not your checkout.
Is ThemesMotion a template marketplace? No. It's a creator discovery and promotion platform. Creators list their templates, and visitors click through to the creator's own checkout.
Should I sell on several platforms? Usually, but gradually. One or two places to sell and a few places to be seen is plenty to start.

Final thoughts

Where to sell website templates is a distribution decision. Big marketplaces give you shoppers and competition. Your own store gives you control and responsibility for traffic. Discovery platforms give you attention that you turn into visits.
Don't ask only "where can I upload my template?" Ask "where are my buyers already looking, and where can they see how good my work is?" Answer that, and the platform list shrinks to a manageable few.
Platform fees, approval rules and features change often. Always check each platform's current terms before you decide.
